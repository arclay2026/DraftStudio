Design Basics, lesson four. Hierarchy.

People don't read designs; they scan them. In a second or two they decide what it's about and whether to look closer. Visual hierarchy is how you control that scan: you decide what people see first, second and third, using size, weight, colour, contrast and position.

Part one: What the eye sees first.

Eye-tracking studies, where cameras record exactly where people look, show the same pattern again and again. Our eyes jump first to the biggest, darkest or most colourful thing, then to faces and pictures, then to whatever stands out next.

So every design should have a planned reading order. On a poster that's usually: 1, the headline (what is it?); 2, the picture (what does it look like?); 3, the details (when, where, how much?); 4, the action (what should I do?).

Before you design, write that order down. Then make sure step 1 really is the most noticeable thing on the page.

Figure one: A planned reading order. Warm areas show where attention lands.

Part two: Size and weight rank information.

Size is the strongest tool you have. The bigger something is, the more important it looks. Weight (how bold it is) is next.

Give each level of information a clearly different size or weight. If two things are almost the same size, the reader can't tell which matters more, and the design feels flat. A useful test: if you have to ask “is this a heading?”, the difference isn't big enough.

Three or four levels are plenty for most designs: a headline, a subheading, body text and small details.

Figure two: The same text at one size, then ranked. The numbers show the new reading order.

Part three: Colour and contrast for emphasis.

Colour attracts attention, but only when it's rare. If everything is bright, nothing stands out.

Keep most of your design in calm colours (white, greys, black, or soft tints) and save your strongest colour for the most important thing, usually the main button. This is the accent from the sixty, thirty, ten rule in the Colour lesson.

Contrast works in both directions: a dark card among light cards stands out just as much as a bright one among dark cards. What matters is being different from the surroundings.

Figure three: Three price cards. Contrast and one blue button make the middle plan the obvious choice.

Part four: Scanning patterns: F and Z.

When people scan, their eyes follow predictable paths. On text-heavy pages like articles and search results, they read in an F pattern: across the top, across a little lower, then down the left side, reading only the first words of each line. So put the most important words at the start of headings and list items.

On simple pages with little text, like adverts, posters and landing pages, the eye follows a Z pattern: top left to top right, diagonally down, then across the bottom. Put your logo at the start of the Z and your button at the end.

Figure four: The two scanning paths. Design along them instead of against them.

Part five: Test it: the squint test.

Here's a test professional designers use daily. Step back from your design and squint, or blur it in your design tool. The details disappear and only the big shapes remain.

What's still visible is your real hierarchy. If you can still see the headline and the button, the design works. If you see a blur of equal grey blocks, or the wrong thing stands out (a decoration, a logo, a photo of a hand), go back and make the important parts bigger, bolder or more contrasting.

Figure five: Blurred, only the headline and the button survive: a clear hierarchy.

Pro tip: Show your design to a friend for five seconds, then hide it. Ask what it was about and what they were supposed to do. If they can't say, the hierarchy needs work.

Common mistakes.

Making everything big and bold, so nothing stands out.

Two competing focal points of the same size.

Using the accent colour on many things instead of one.

Hiding the main button in a corner or in a weak colour.

Recap.

Plan a reading order: what's first, second, third.

Size and weight rank information; make the differences obvious.

Save your strongest colour for the one main action.

Design along the F and Z scanning paths.

Squint or blur to test what really stands out.

Practise it: Make a simple event poster. Write down the reading order first, then use size, weight and one accent colour to make it happen. That's the end of this lesson.
