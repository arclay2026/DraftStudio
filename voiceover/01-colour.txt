Design Basics, lesson one. Colour.

Colour is the first thing people notice, often before they read a single word. It sets the mood, tells people what kind of brand you are, and points their eyes to what matters. The good news: you don't need “an eye for colour”. Designers use a few simple tools and rules, and in this lesson you'll learn all of them.

Part one: The colour wheel.

The colour wheel arranges hues (the pure colours: red, orange, yellow and so on) in a circle, in the same order you see in a rainbow. Our wheel has 12 hues, each 30 degrees apart. Designers use it like a map: where two colours sit on the wheel tells you how they will behave together.

Neighbours (hues side by side) share a base colour, so they always blend smoothly. Opposites (hues straight across the wheel) are as different as two colours can be, so they create strong contrast and energy.

The wheel also splits into a warm half (reds, oranges, yellows) and a cool half (greens, blues, violets). Warm colours feel energetic, friendly and urgent, which is why food brands and sales use them. Cool colours feel calm, clean and trustworthy, which is why banks, health and tech brands lean blue.

Figure one: The 12-hue wheel. The dashed line joins two opposites: red and cyan.

Part two: Hue, saturation and lightness.

Every colour on a screen can be described with three numbers, called H S L. Learning them makes choosing colours much less of a guessing game.

Hue is which colour it is, as a position on the wheel from 0 degrees to 360 degrees (0 degrees is red, 120 degrees green, 240 degrees blue). Saturation is how strong or vivid it is, from 0 percent (grey) to 100 percent (as intense as possible). Lightness is how light or dark it is, from 0 percent (black) to 100 percent (white).

Here's the trick professionals use: keep the hue, change the other two. A brand blue at 55 percent lightness for buttons, the same hue at 95 percent for a soft background, and at 15 percent for dark text. They all match, because they are the same colour family.

Figure two: The three dials of a colour. The card on the right breaks one blue into its three numbers.

Pro tip: Muddy, dull colours usually have low saturation in the middle of the lightness range. Fix them by raising saturation or moving lightness up or down.

Part three: Colour harmonies.

A harmony is a recipe for picking colours that look good together, based on their positions on the wheel. Four recipes cover almost every design.

Complementary: two opposite hues, like blue and orange. Bold and eye-catching; let one colour lead and use the other for small accents. Analogous: three neighbours, like pink, red and orange. Calm and natural, great for backgrounds and gentle brands.

Triadic: three hues spaced evenly (120 degrees apart), like red, green and blue. Playful and balanced; works best when one hue dominates. Split complementary: one hue plus the two hues next to its opposite. It has the punch of complementary but is easier to balance, which makes it a great default.

Figure three: Each recipe as dots on the wheel, and a five-colour palette built from it (the hues plus a light and a dark).

Part four: The sixty, thirty, ten rule.

Even a perfect palette looks messy if every colour is used equally. The sixty, thirty, ten rule fixes that by giving each colour a job and a share of the space.

About 60 percent goes to a dominant colour, usually the background and large areas. It sets the mood. About 30 percent goes to a secondary colour for text, image panels and cards. It builds structure. The last 10 percent is the accent: buttons, badges, a highlighted word. Because it's rare, the accent is what people notice.

The numbers are a guide, not maths homework. The point is uneven amounts: one colour clearly in charge, one supporting, one that pops.

Figure four: A café poster: cream sets the mood, deep green gives structure, orange marks the action.

Part five: Contrast keeps text readable.

Contrast is the difference in lightness between text and its background. It's measured as a ratio, from 1 to 1 (no difference) to 21 to 1 (black on white). The web accessibility guidelines (W C A G) ask for at least 4.5 to 1 for normal text and 3 to 1 for large headings.

Hue doesn't count, only lightness. Red text on a green background can look vivid but be almost unreadable, because the two are equally light. Light grey on white is the most common mistake on the web: it looks elegant on a big bright monitor and disappears on a phone in the sun.

Check every text colour you use. Most design tools, and many free websites, show the ratio instantly when you enter two colours.

Figure five: Six real pairs and their measured ratios. Green ticks pass for body text.

Common mistakes.

Using five colours in equal amounts, so nothing stands out.

Grey text on white that's too light to read.

Pure, fully saturated colours everywhere; they tire the eye. Tone most of them down.

Choosing colours you like instead of colours that suit the brand and its customers.

Recap.

Hues sit on a wheel: neighbours blend, opposites contrast.

Describe colours with hue, saturation and lightness, and keep one hue across light and dark shades.

Use a harmony recipe to pick a palette.

Share space sixty, thirty, ten so one colour leads and the accent pops.

Keep text contrast at 4.5 to 1 or more.

Practise it: Pick a base colour, try each harmony, and check the text contrast it shows for every swatch. That's the end of this lesson.
